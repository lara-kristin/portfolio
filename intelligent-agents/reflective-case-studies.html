Reflective Case Studies

Three reflective case studies from my research agent project.

Case Study 1: Bias from Unequal Metadata Availability

While testing my research agent, I found that Semantic Scholar did not provide abstracts consistently across retrieved papers. Because the Evaluation Agent relies on abstract text for relevance scoring, treating a paper without an abstract as weak would have penalised missing metadata rather than its content. I therefore excluded such papers from automated relevance scoring, while keeping them visible to the researcher and reporting the missing abstracts in the brief's limitations. This avoided one form of unequal treatment while accepting another: a paper without an abstract cannot enter the proposed evidence set, however relevant it might be.

This showed me that bias can arise from the information surrounding an autonomous decision, not only from the model itself. Mehrabi et al. (2021) describe measurement bias as arising from how features are selected and measured. A related pattern appeared in verification: AI-focused searches returned many arXiv preprints, whereas a biology topic produced many Crossref-verifiable records. The same control therefore had different reach because of publishing conventions rather than paper quality.

In future, I would test such controls across domains, distinguish unavailable from negative evidence, and provide a separate review route for unassessable records.

Reference

Mehrabi, N. et al. (2021) ‘A survey on bias and fairness in machine learning’, ACM Computing Surveys, 54(6), pp. 1–35. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1145/3457607

Case Study 2: Bounded Autonomy and the Placement of Human Oversight

I designed my research agent with two human checkpoints to bound autonomy. The first allows unlimited revision before retrieval; the second permits one scope revision after evidence review because later correction repeats retrieval, validation and assessment. The system may also reformulate a query once when retrieval is insufficient, capped because this changes an approved plan without asking the researcher again.

Zhu et al. (2026) distinguish AI operative agency from human evaluative agency, where humans judge, verify and steer AI outputs. I placed the second gate before synthesis so the researcher could decide what evidence should be used. However, the summaries are generated without further human approval. The system checks that each summary names an approved paper, but not that its claims follow from that paper's abstract, even though summaries can contain content unsupported by their source (Maynez et al., 2020). This showed me that I had bounded autonomy mainly around correction cost rather than error consequence.

In future, I would retain the evidence checkpoint but add a post-synthesis faithfulness check, placing safeguards according to risk as well as workflow cost.

References

Maynez, J. et al. (2020) 'On faithfulness and factuality in abstractive summarization', Proceedings of the 58th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, Online. Association for Computational Linguistics, pp. 1906-1919. Available at: https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.173

Zhu, L. et al. (2026) 'Designing meaningful human oversight in AI', AI and Ethics, 6, article 286. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-026-01147-7

Case Study 3: The Limits of Explainable Decisions

I designed my research agent to make relevance decisions inspectable. Each selected paper shows its relevance score, verification status and addressed sub-questions, while the JSON retains all assessments, including rejected papers and the model's stated reason for each score.

I initially treated this as explainability. However, these fields do not have equal evidential status. Crossref verification is independently determined, whereas relevance scores, sub-question attribution and reasons depend on model judgement. The reason is generated alongside the score, so it is a model-produced justification rather than a record of how the score was reached. Parcalabescu and Frank (2024) argue that plausible natural-language explanations may still be unfaithful to the reasoning behind a prediction. I also found that scores clustered on round values; in one run, three pairings scored 1.00 and two scored 0.60, suggesting coarse bands rather than precise measurements. My system can therefore show what it decided and what it says about why, but not how the decision was produced.

In future, I would separate traceability from explanation and ground relevance explanations in observable evidence, such as supporting abstract passages.

References

Parcalabescu, L. and Frank, A. (2024) 'On measuring faithfulness or self-consistency of natural language explanations', Proceedings of the 62nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers). Bangkok, Thailand. Association for Computational Linguistics, pp. 6048-6089. Available at: https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.329
