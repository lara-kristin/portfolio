Collaborative Discussion 1: Agent-Based Systems

Discussion Task

Discuss what has led to the rise of agent-based systems and the benefits that this approach can offer to organisations. Discussion should include at least three references.

Additional Task

You should research how LLM-powered agents are designed for academic research or information gathering. Find at least one academic paper, technical article, or credible online source that explains agent architectures, task decomposition, or planning strategies.

In your discussion post, summarise the key ideas from your research, explain how these ideas could support your own agent design, and describe one design decision you plan to make for your project.

You should also reply to at least two classmates, comparing approaches and offering constructive suggestions.

Initial Post

The growing use of agent-based systems reflects both the increasing complexity of organisational processes and continued advances in artificial intelligence. Many modern tasks are distributed, constantly changing and difficult to manage through a single centralised system. Multi-agent systems respond to this challenge by dividing work among autonomous, specialised agents that can interpret their environment, make decisions and coordinate their actions. More recently, large language models have accelerated this development by improving agents’ ability to communicate in natural language, reason, plan, retain information and access external knowledge (Li et al., 2024).

Agent-based computing also builds on established AI research. For example, the Belief-Desire-Intention architecture represents what an agent knows, what it aims to achieve and the plans it chooses to follow. This allows agents to act purposefully in changing environments. However, Archibald et al. (2024) show that performance is strongly influenced by how agents prioritise competing intentions, indicating that autonomy alone does not guarantee effective decision-making.

For organisations, agent-based systems can support the division of tasks, parallel problem-solving and decentralised decision-making. Organisational models can also define roles and communication protocols to structure how agents interact. However, these advantages can come with trade-offs. Bouslimi (2024) found that an organisation-based multi-agent system remained highly efficient as task complexity increased, although specialised roles and rigid communication protocols limited its flexibility and robustness. Overall, agent-based systems can provide considerable value for organisations managing complex and distributed work, but their effectiveness depends on strong coordination, appropriate validation and resilient system design.

Additional Task

Research into STORM and PaSa suggests that LLM-powered research systems should separate planning, retrieval and evaluation rather than respond immediately using only the language model’s existing knowledge.

STORM decomposes a broad topic into several research perspectives, generates follow-up questions, retrieves information from external sources and organises the collected evidence into an outline before producing a long-form response (Shao et al., 2024). PaSa focuses specifically on academic paper search. It uses a Crawler to generate search queries, retrieve papers and follow citation links, while a Selector assesses whether each paper satisfies the user’s requirements (He et al., 2025).

Both systems demonstrate the value of task decomposition, iterative searching and evaluating retrieved information before it is used in the final research output. These principles could inform our group’s agent design by enabling the system to expand or redirect its search when the initial results are insufficient or irrelevant. One design option to consider is a single coordinating planning agent that converts the user’s goal into focused subquestions and manages separate stages for retrieval, relevance assessment, evidence extraction and synthesis. This architecture could help maintain alignment with the overall research goal while supporting a modular, transparent and traceable workflow.

Peer Contributions

Response to Anne

Hi Anne,

Thank you for raising this point. Your distinction between error propagation and restriction neglect made me reconsider whether planning and revision should remain concentrated within a single agent. It also gives me a useful perspective to bring to my group when we discuss whether a single coordinating agent or a more distributed multi-agent architecture would be most appropriate.

I would be open to a distributed architecture in which planning, retrieval and evaluation are handled by separate but interacting agents. Li et al. (2024) describe LLM-based multi-agent systems as involving specialised roles, inter-agent communication and the adjustment of plans through feedback and reflection. More specifically, Deng et al. (2025) propose an iterative framework containing a query planner, retrieval agent and relevance evaluator, demonstrating how these functions can remain distinct while exchanging information throughout the search process.

Within such an architecture, the retrieval agent could flag limited search coverage or insufficient results, while the evaluation agent could identify poorly matched or low-relevance evidence. Both could recommend revised queries or subquestions rather than allowing the system to continue with an ineffective plan. I would therefore allow retrieval and evaluation agents to challenge the plan rather than keeping every revision exclusively with the planner.

However, they should not be able to change the original research goal or remove stated constraints independently. The planner should maintain an explicit record of the user’s requirements, while significant revisions should return to the user for approval. This aligns with Borghoff, Bottoni and Pareschi (2025), whose multi-agent framework gives human participants responsibility for guiding overall objectives while LLMs and specialised agents perform coordinated supporting roles.

Your point therefore highlights a useful middle ground: specialised agents can provide operational feedback and initiate replanning, while the planner and user retain oversight of changes that could affect the original research requirements.

Response to Isaac

Hi Isaac,

Your post provides a balanced overview of both the benefits and risks of agent-based systems. I particularly agree that greater autonomy does not necessarily produce greater value. However, I wonder whether adaptability and coordination provide a sufficiently clear basis for deciding when agent-based computing is appropriate.

Recent evidence suggests that the value of multi-agent coordination is highly task-dependent. Kim et al. (2026) found substantial performance gains for multi-agent systems in structured financial reasoning, but considerable deterioration in sequential planning tasks. They argue that coordination can become counterproductive when its complexity exceeds that of the underlying task. Similarly, Mandal et al. (2025) found that a multi-agent framework outperformed a single-agent configuration in laboratory automation. However, the LLM agents still struggled with basic tasks, sometimes deviated from instructions, and both configurations remained sensitive to minor changes in prompting. Improved relative performance therefore does not necessarily establish sufficient reliability for organisational deployment.

Tool use creates an additional challenge. Xu et al. (2025) explain that agents must determine whether a tool is needed, identify the appropriate tool and invoke it correctly across multiple steps. For simple tasks, errors in tool retrieval and parameter selection may even create more difficulty than completing the task directly.

I would therefore argue that organisations should not adopt agent-based systems primarily because a process is dynamic or requires coordination. They should first benchmark multi-agent, single-agent and deterministic alternatives using task-specific measures such as accuracy, robustness, cost and recoverability. How should organisations determine when agentic flexibility genuinely outweighs the additional coordination and reliability risks?

Response to Connor

Hi Connor,

Your post picks up on how quickly AI agent systems have been taken up, and on how widely they are now being applied. The point about cross-industry deployment seems right to me, and the organisational potential is hard to dispute. What I keep circling back to is whether that same versatility makes limited human oversight a benefit, or simply a by-product of how these systems are built.

Acharya, Kuppan and Divya (2025) describe agentic AI as capable of pursuing complex goals with minimal human intervention, but they also identify challenges surrounding goal alignment, resource constraints and safe integration. This suggests that increasing autonomy may create organisational value, but also introduces risks that need to be managed rather than simply reducing the need for human involvement.

This becomes particularly important in higher-risk applications. Zhao et al. (2026), for example, identify considerable potential for AI agents across healthcare, including diagnosis, clinical decision-making and hospital operations. At the same time, they highlight persistent challenges involving hallucinations, limited interpretability and unclear accountability. The consequences of these limitations are likely to differ considerably depending on whether an agent is automating a routine administrative task or contributing to a clinical decision.

Rather than treating autonomy and human oversight as opposites, Zhu et al. (2026) propose distinguishing between AI operative agency and human evaluative agency. Under this approach, agents can retain autonomy in performing tasks while humans remain responsible for evaluating, steering or overriding their outputs when necessary.

I therefore wonder whether reduced human oversight should itself be viewed as an organisational benefit, or whether the appropriate level of autonomy should instead depend on the risk and consequences associated with a particular task.

References

Acharya, D. B., Kuppan, K. and Divya, B. (2025) 'Agentic AI: autonomous intelligence for complex goals—a comprehensive survey', IEEE Access, 13, pp. 18912-18936. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3532853

Zhao, L. et al. (2026) 'AI agent in healthcare: applications, evaluations, and future directions', npj Artificial Intelligence, 2, article 31. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1038/s44387-026-00076-4

Zhu, L. et al. (2026) 'Designing meaningful human oversight in AI', AI and Ethics, 6, article 286. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-026-01147-7
