Correlation and Regression

Exploring the relationship between population and GDP per capita using correlation analysis and linear regression.

Activity Overview

This seminar activity investigated whether population size is related to GDP per capita. Population and GDP datasets were cleaned, matched by country and combined before calculating mean population and mean GDP per capita. Pearson correlation was then used to examine the strength of the linear relationship, followed by a simple linear regression model using population as the predictor.

Analysis and Findings

Preparing the data required more than simply joining the two datasets. The GDP file extended only to 2020 while the population file extended to 2021, and both datasets contained entries representing regions and other country groups rather than individual countries. A total of 49 such aggregate entries were removed. Countries with insufficient observations were then excluded using a minimum requirement of ten years of GDP-per-capita data. This removed nine countries and territories and resulted in a final dataset of 208.

The analysis found practically no linear relationship between mean population and mean GDP per capita. Pearson's correlation coefficient was -0.067 with a p-value of 0.336, meaning the observed correlation was very weak and not statistically significant. The scatter plot also showed substantial variation in GDP per capita among countries with similar population sizes.

The linear regression model confirmed this result. Population explained only 0.3% of the variation in GDP per capita in the training data (R² = 0.003). Test performance was slightly worse than predicting the average value, with an R² of -0.016 and a mean absolute error of approximately $18,200. Population alone was therefore not a useful predictor of GDP per capita in this dataset.

Reflection

This activity reinforced that a statistically appropriate workflow begins with understanding and preparing the data rather than immediately fitting a model. Decisions such as removing aggregate entries, defining a minimum amount of available data and handling different time ranges affected which countries were represented in the final analysis.

The exercise also highlighted an ethical aspect of data preparation. The rule requiring at least ten years of GDP-per-capita data was applied consistently, but it did not affect all countries equally. Several countries with limited data, including Somalia, South Sudan and North Korea, were excluded, meaning that some poorer countries became underrepresented in the final dataset. This showed me that even technically reasonable cleaning decisions can introduce selection or representation bias and influence whose circumstances are reflected in an analysis.

I also learned that fitting a regression model does not mean that the predictor is useful. The near-zero correlation and poor R² values showed clearly that population alone provides almost no explanatory power for GDP per capita. The exercise therefore highlighted the importance of evaluating data quality, representation, statistical relationships and model performance before drawing conclusions.

Limitations

The GDP data ended in 2020 while population data extended to 2021, so the averaging periods were not identical. The missing-data rule also excluded nine countries and territories, including some relatively poor countries, meaning that missing data was not random and the final sample may slightly underrepresent poorer countries. In addition, Pearson correlation and simple linear regression capture only linear relationships and can be affected by extreme observations such as very large countries and small high-income states.
